## Summary
Qualitative interview study exploring how software practitioners perceive and manage technical debt (TD) in real world industrial settings. Based on interviews with 35 professionals across various roles and organisations, the paper characterises TD as a "balancing act" between short term delivery pressures and long term system health. It identifies that while practitioners are acutely aware of debt, they often lack formal mechanisms to track it, relying instead on "gut feeling" and informal communication.

## Key Arguments
- Practitioners define TD broadly, encompassing not just code issues but also testing, documentation, and architectural deficiencies
- Most debt is incurred intentionally to meet market deadlines, though unintentional debt (due to lack of skill or changing requirements) remains a significant factor
- There is a major disconnect between the awareness of TD and the formal tracking of it; debt is rarely documented in backlogs until it becomes a critical "blocker"
- The "interest" on debt is perceived as a decrease in developer morale and an increase in the unpredictability of release schedules

## Key Concepts
- Practitioner perception
- Intentional vs Unintentional debt
- Debt tracking vs Debt awareness
- Interest manifestation (friction)
- Decision making trade offs

## Relevance to Project
- Section 2.5.3 (Expanding the Conceptual Perimeter): Provides empirical evidence for how the boundaries of TD expand in practice to include non-code artefacts like documentation and testing.

- Section 2.9.2 (Cross-Cutting Limitations): Illustrates the industry-wide struggle to move from qualitative awareness to quantitative measurement, reinforcing the research gap my project aims to fill.
